# Grassroots

A political career simulator in the spirit of *The Political Process*. Pick any state, city and ZIP code in the United States, build a candidate, and climb from school board to the White House: win primaries and general elections against hundreds of AI politicians, write laws, balance budgets, fight your way up the leadership ladder, and raise a family that can carry on your dynasty.

## Play

Open `index.html` in a browser. No build step or server is needed. Games save in your browser's local storage, and the Menu can copy a save code you can paste on the title screen anywhere.

For a local server instead: `python3 -m http.server` in this folder, then visit http://localhost:8000.

## How it plays

- **Start anywhere.** Choose a state on the US map, then enter any real residential ZIP code (about 33,000 of them) or pick a city. Your ZIP decides your city council ward, school board district, state house and state senate districts, and congressional district. Districts are drawn from real ZIP code populations, and each one has its own partisan lean and profile: population, density, income, poverty, education, age, veterans, gun owners, union members and more.
- **Build a character.** Name, gender, age, party (Democrat or Republican), a cartoon portrait, upbringing, education, occupation, two traits, five skills (charisma, debate, policy, fundraising, grassroots) and positions on 54 policies across 12 categories. Your fiscal and social ideology and caucus follow from those positions.
- **Weeks and hours.** Each Next Turn is one week. A day job costs about 18 hours of your 40. Spend the rest campaigning, governing, networking, studying, dating or with your family.
- **Nine offices.** School board, city council, mayor, state house, state senate, U.S. House, governor, U.S. Senate and President, each on its real election cycle (local races in odd years, governors on each state's schedule, Senate seats by class, filing deadlines in March, primaries in June, generals in November).
- **Campaigns.** The tabs follow the classic layout: Overview (campaign manager, opportunities, finances, name recognition, voter enthusiasm by party, effect on public opinion, Predict Election), Events (rallies, town halls, door-knocking by county or neighborhood, donor calls, fundraisers, interviews, debate prep, opposition research), Marketing (TV, radio, digital and mail ads with positive, contrast or attack tone), Opponents, Poll Data, Finance, Contacts (endorsements from interest groups and officeholders), Staff, Policy (promote positions to move each party's enthusiasm), Volunteers (offices that recruit and canvass) and a Campaign Map colored by projected margin.
- **Votes come from voters.** Every district is a mix of Democrats, Republicans and independents. Each group weighs party loyalty, how much your positions excite them, name recognition, money, ground game, endorsements, incumbency, debates and scandals. Results are reported county by county or neighborhood by neighborhood.
- **A living world.** Every seat in your state (all of New Hampshire's 400 House seats included) is held by an AI politician with a career, ideology, traits and ambitions. They retire, run for higher office, lose primaries and get appointed to vacancies. Other states' congressional and Senate races follow the national environment, and the presidency is contested state by state.
- **Governing runs on relationships and respect.** Colleagues vote on your bills based on their views, their districts, their party *and how much they like and respect you*. Build relationships over coffee, at caucus meetings, by co-sponsoring and voting for their bills, campaigning for them and donating from your war chest. Ask for pledges, check the whip count, trade votes, and keep your word, or watch respect collapse.
- **Leadership.** After each election, run for Assistant Whip, Whip, Caucus Chair, Majority or Minority Leader, Speaker or President pro tempore. Caucus members vote one by one based on their relationship with you and your rival. Leaders control which bills reach the floor, and committee chairs decide what advances.
- **Public finance once you're elected.** Every government runs a real fiscal engine. Statehouses and governors work with that state's actual agencies (Caltrans, CDCR, Medi-Cal and CalFresh in California; TxDOT, TDCJ and HHSC in Texas) and its real legislature size. Congress and the President work at federal scale: all 435 Representatives and 100 Senators as individual lawmakers, and 19 federal agencies (DOD, HHS, SSA, DOT, DOE and more). Each agency has operating and capital lines, a degradation index, an audit rating and public approval. Entitlements have adjustable eligibility, benefit caps, co-pays and COLAs: Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, SNAP, the ACA, the child tax credit, TANF, state EITCs, pensions and more.
- **A macro calculator with no built-in ideology.** Each quarter runs the economy. Fiscal impulses use short-run multipliers and leakage, and feed an output gap. Unemployment follows a Phillips curve, and so does inflation. A Taylor rule sets interest rates, and bond yields respond to debt and a dynamic credit-rating ladder. Taxes reduce private liquidity and carry behavioral responses. Capital spending on infrastructure, R&D and education builds public capital stocks that raise potential growth with diminishing returns. Inefficient or deficit-financed spending pushes up inflation, yields and debt service. States and localities must balance their budgets, first from reserves and then with automatic across-the-board cuts.
- **Crises & Needs Matrix.** Each state starts with its real structural problems: housing, homelessness and out-migration in Hawaii and California, education and health deficits in Mississippi, pension debt in Illinois, and so on. Federal play adds the trust funds, debt sustainability and the border. Crises respond to agency funding, entitlement settings, taxes and laws. If ignored, they drive voter dissatisfaction, caucus rebellion and the credit rating.
- **Bill Constructor and a procedural docket.** Bills combine tax and regulatory changes (brackets, corporate, capital gains, payroll, sales, property, carbon, plus compliance costs by industry), line-item appropriations by agency (operations and capital), entitlement triggers, earmarks and structural tools such as privatization, public-private partnerships and buy-domestic mandates. A budget office scores each bill dynamically over 5–10 years. AI legislation is dealt like a deck of cards: an ideological faction (Progressive through Populist) × a tool × a real agency × a live crisis. The docket never repeats.
- **Vectorized votes.** Every lawmaker scores every bill as 0.3 × ideological tax alignment + 0.3 × district demographics and local industry impact + 0.2 × agency efficiency/audit ratings + 0.2 × political capital and earmarks. Members judge taxes against their own ideal rate, then caucuses vote as blocs and party whips pull. Roll calls show every member's four components. The U.S. Senate needs 60 votes for cloture unless the bill is budget reconciliation. Budgets have deadlines, and a missed one brings a continuing resolution or a shutdown.
- **Committees are the gatekeepers.** At the start of each session the party steering committee hands out seats. Every member sits on at least two committees, with a limit on exclusive panels such as Appropriations and Ways & Means/Finance. Win seats by spending political capital and trading favors with leadership: a loyalty pledge (break it twice and you lose a seat), party dues, or carrying the leader's bill. Committee seniority decides who becomes chair and ranking member, but you can bid for a gavel. A bill reaches the floor only if its chair schedules a hearing and the committee votes it out after markup. Chairs have agenda control: they can fast-track, hold or table anything. Members get the exclusive markup phase: earmarks for their own district or for an undecided colleague, crisis amendments that fund the lever behind an active crisis, rewrites, strikes and pay-fors. Each amendment goes to a committee vote and is re-scored. Lawmakers off the committee must lobby the chair, cut a deal, or file a discharge petition.
- **The governing turn.** Office → Briefing has four parts: an office and agency status briefing with the crisis matrix, the systemic economic and fiscal ledger, the agency budget and compliance report, and an action menu. The menu covers the Bill Constructor, committee power, backroom negotiations with chairs and swing votes, emergency fund transfers, audits, and advancing to the next quarter.
- **Every state and city plays differently.** Each of the 50 states and D.C. has a dossier of what it is actually dealing with: its own power players (Texas oil and gas and border hawks, Michigan's UAW, Utah's LDS Church, Nevada's casinos and the Culinary Union, Florida's retirees and Big Sugar), the companies circling it (TSMC in Arizona, Hyundai in Georgia, Micron in New York), the real megaprojects on its table (California High-Speed Rail, the Gateway tunnel, the Texas Water Fund, the Key Bridge rebuild), and decision scenes about its real issues (Arizona's shrinking Colorado River share, Florida's insurance collapse, Illinois' pension ramp, Jackson's water system). About 50 major cities get their own dossiers too (Chicago's budget hole, LA's fire rebuild, the Kensington drug market in Philadelphia, the Royals stadium fight in Kansas City). Smaller places get scenes built from their own data: their county, their agencies and their worst crises.
- **Power bases.** Once in office you answer to a mix of bases that depends on your office (parents and teachers on a school board; neighborhoods, police and business in a city; party, donors and local officials in a statehouse; the military and seniors in Washington) plus the local power players from your dossier. Loyalty drifts toward targets set by conditions, your positions and your record. Loyal bases raise approval, money and political capital; hostile ones fund primary challengers, organize recalls, leak, and in the worst case open impeachment inquiries or ousters from leadership.
- **Decisions & events.** Scenes interrupt the week with real choices, and every choice shows what it moved (approval, bases, crises, agencies, money). Executives decide; legislators take a position, so their effect on government scales with their power. Each January executives give a State of the City, State of the State or State of the Union address built from the year's record.
- **Cabinet & staff.** Mayors appoint their police chief and public works director; governors appoint the people running their real agencies (Caltrans, TxDOT, CDCR); presidents name a full cabinet that the Senate must confirm. Appointees have competence, loyalty and integrity, run their agencies better or worse, and can leak or become scandals. Legislators hire a chief of staff, legislative director, communications director and district director.
- **Economic development, from both sides.** Executives court companies with incentive packages built around what each company wants, and launch megaprojects: feasibility study, competing financing (bonds, referendums, federal grants, P3s, TIF, team owners), construction with overruns and lawsuits, and a ribbon cutting. Big deals and bond packages go to the council, legislature or Congress as real authorization bills. AI mayors, governors and presidents run the same system, so as a legislator you vote on their deals, pitch your district for the next plant, demand local-hiring agreements, or denounce corporate welfare. Senators vote on confirmations and committee members hold oversight hearings on real agencies.
- **Presidential runs.** Nominating contests from Iowa to the final primaries with proportional delegates, a convention, a running mate, and an Electoral College map.
- **Life.** Date, marry, have or adopt kids, keep your family close (or not), manage money, health and stress, go back to school, change jobs, move. When you die or retire, an adult child can carry on the family name.

## Code layout

```
index.html              entry point
css/style.css           visual system (manila folder tabs, black-banded tables; light and dark)
js/util.js              helpers, seeded random numbers, calendar
js/data/                states, 54 policy positions, characters, offices, leadership, committees,
                        real state & federal agencies, tax structures, the crises & needs matrix,
                        regional_*.js (state and city dossiers)
js/geo/                 per-state ZIP, city and county data plus map outlines (loaded on demand)
js/engine/              geography and districts, world, elections, campaign, government, presidency,
                        debates, life, events, weekly turn, save/load;
                        fiscal.js (public finance & macro), legis.js (bills, scoring, votes),
                        billgen.js (procedural policy deck), committees.js (steering, gavels, markup),
                        power.js (power bases), scenes.js (decisions, threats, yearly address),
                        develop.js (companies & megaprojects), cabinet.js (appointees & staff)
js/ui/                  rendering core and portraits, character creation, game shell, tabs;
                        govern.js (briefing, Bill Constructor, docket, committees, negotiations, roll calls),
                        exec.js (power bases, cabinet, development)
tools/                  scripts that build the state and geography data files
```

The engine has no DOM dependencies, so it loads in Node for headless testing.

## Data

ZIP code, city, county and population data come from the SimpleMaps US ZIP code database (CC BY 4.0), via the `zipcode-detail-lookup` package. State and county outlines come from the U.S. Census Bureau via `us-atlas` (ISC). Partisan leans, legislature sizes, salaries and election cycles are approximate mid-2020s values. All politicians in the game are fictional. This is a game, not a forecast.
